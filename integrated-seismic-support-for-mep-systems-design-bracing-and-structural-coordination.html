Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems: Design, Bracing, and Structural Coordination

anitaa September 13, 2026

Modern commercial, healthcare, industrial, and infrastructure buildings depend on extensive mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems to remain functional. HVAC equipment, ductwork, piping, electrical conduit, cable trays, pumps, generators, and other MEP components are typically distributed throughout the building and connected to its structural frame. During an earthquake, however, these systems can experience forces and movement that are substantially different from their normal gravity and operating loads. That makes seismic support an engineering issue that extends beyond simply selecting stronger hangers or brackets.

integrated seismic support for mep systems brings seismic bracing, restraint, anchorage, structural attachment, vibration control, BIM coordination, and fabrication into a coordinated design process. Instead of treating every MEP trade as an independent installation, an integrated approach considers how supported components interact with the building structure, adjacent systems, equipment movement, and the available load path.

For engineers and contractors, this distinction is important. A seismic brace is only one part of a complete restraint system. The brace member, connection hardware, support assembly, anchor, structural attachment, and supporting structure must work together to transfer applicable seismic forces safely. At the same time, the system must accommodate operational requirements such as thermal expansion, vibration, maintenance access, and equipment serviceability.

In the United States, seismic MEP support design is commonly developed within the project’s applicable building-code and structural-engineering framework. ASCE 7 provides important provisions for seismic design of nonstructural components, while the IBC and applicable state and local codes establish the broader regulatory framework. California projects may also involve the CBC and, for healthcare facilities, HCAI requirements formerly associated with OSHPD.

A technically sound solution therefore begins with project-specific seismic criteria and ends with coordinated engineering, fabrication, installation, and field verification. This article examines how that process works across HVAC, piping, plumbing, electrical systems, and mechanical equipment, including the role of structural engineering, BIM 3D CAD, custom metal fabrication, and seismic restraint coordination.

What Is Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems?

Integrated seismic support is an engineered approach to supporting and restraining mechanical, electrical, and plumbing components so that applicable earthquake-induced forces can be transferred through a defined structural load path. It combines conventional support functions with seismic bracing, restraint, anchorage, and structural attachment requirements.

A conventional MEP support may primarily be designed to carry gravity loads such as the weight of a pipe, duct, cable tray, or piece of equipment. Seismic support introduces additional considerations. When the building moves laterally during an earthquake, supported components have mass and therefore develop inertial forces. Their geometry, connection configuration, support spacing, flexibility, and attachment to the structure all influence how those forces are transmitted.

Conventional MEP Supports vs. Seismic Supports

A pipe hanger, trapeze, equipment base, or strut-channel assembly can support weight without necessarily providing adequate seismic restraint. Seismic support systems may incorporate dedicated brace members, rigid connections, lateral restraints, anchors, and structural attachments intended to control component movement.

This does not mean every component requires the same configuration. The applicable design depends on project seismic criteria, component characteristics, support geometry, and code requirements. Engineers may evaluate whether a component requires transverse restraint, longitudinal restraint, anchorage, or another engineered configuration.

Why an Integrated Approach Matters

MEP systems occupy the same physical environment. A duct may cross above a pipe rack, a cable tray may pass beside a brace, and a seismic support may need to connect to a structural beam that is also needed by another trade. Designing each support independently can create clashes, inaccessible anchors, inefficient load paths, or field modifications.

An integrated MEP seismic support strategy coordinates these relationships before installation. This is particularly valuable in mechanical rooms, hospitals, data centers, central utility plants, and other facilities where MEP density is high and operational continuity is important.

The result is not simply more hardware. It is a coordinated support system in which seismic engineering, structural attachment, MEP coordination, fabrication, and installation are considered as connected parts of one engineering workflow.

How Seismic Forces Affect MEP Systems

Earthquake motion affects MEP systems because supported components move with the building while their mass tends to resist changes in motion. The resulting inertial response can impose forces on supports, braces, connections, anchors, and the structure supporting them. Understanding this load path is fundamental to effective seismic support design.

Seismic Forces on Nonstructural Components

MEP systems are generally considered nonstructural components even though they can have significant weight and structural consequences. Large air-handling units, chillers, generators, piping networks, cable trays, and other equipment can impose substantial forces on their supports during seismic events.

The seismic response of an MEP component depends on factors such as its weight, location within the building, attachment configuration, building characteristics, and applicable seismic design parameters. The design process therefore should not assume that a generic brace configuration is suitable for every installation.

For example, a suspended pipe system and a floor-mounted generator may both require seismic restraint, but their support geometry and force-transfer mechanisms are fundamentally different. A suspended system may transfer forces through hanger rods, trapeze assemblies, brace members, and structural attachments. A floor-mounted generator may rely more heavily on equipment anchorage and the capacity of its supporting slab or structural frame.

Load Path From MEP Component to Structure

A complete seismic design follows the force from the MEP component through every connection to the building structure. This can be represented as:

MEP component → support assembly → brace or restraint → connection → anchor → structural member → building structure

Any weak link can compromise the intended load path. A brace with adequate capacity does not compensate for an inadequate anchor. Similarly, a properly selected anchor does not solve a structural attachment that has insufficient capacity.

This is why seismic calculations should be coordinated with structural engineering. Concrete anchors may require evaluation under applicable provisions such as ACI 318, while steel support members and structural attachments may require appropriate structural design considerations. Anchor products may also have project-specific evaluation requirements, including applicable ICC-ES documentation.

For existing buildings, field conditions become equally important. The actual location, thickness, reinforcement, structural member geometry, and available attachment points may differ from assumptions in original drawings. Field verification can therefore be an essential part of retrofit seismic support design.

MEP Seismic Bracing Design and Engineering Requirements

MEP seismic bracing design begins with project-specific criteria rather than with a standard hardware selection. Engineers first identify the systems and components subject to applicable seismic provisions, determine the relevant design parameters, and evaluate how forces can be transferred into the building structure.

Seismic Design Criteria

ASCE 7 contains provisions addressing seismic design of nonstructural components, including requirements that can affect mechanical and electrical equipment and their supports. The IBC incorporates seismic design concepts into the building-code framework, while the CBC establishes applicable California requirements.

The exact design methodology depends on the adopted code edition, jurisdiction, project classification, seismic design category, component characteristics, and other project-specific parameters. For that reason, seismic support drawings should not be treated as universally interchangeable between projects.

Transverse and Longitudinal Bracing

For suspended piping, ductwork, and other elongated systems, engineers may evaluate restraint in multiple directions. Transverse bracing controls movement across the system, while longitudinal bracing addresses movement along the system. Brace locations and spacing depend on the system configuration and applicable design requirements.

The goal is not simply to make an MEP installation rigid. Restraint must be coordinated with movement requirements, connections, thermal expansion, flexible interfaces, and equipment operation. Excessive rigidity in the wrong location can create its own coordination and mechanical problems.

Structural Attachment and Anchorage

Structural attachment is one of the most critical parts of the load path. A support may connect to concrete, structural steel, or another approved structural element, and each attachment condition requires appropriate engineering evaluation.

Concrete anchors, adhesive anchors, mechanical anchors, welded connections, bolted connections, beam attachments, base plates, gusset plates, and reinforcement plates may all appear in engineered support assemblies. Their selection should reflect the actual substrate, loads, edge distances, installation conditions, and applicable project requirements.

This is where structural engineering and MEP support engineering intersect. Effective integrated seismic support is therefore more than brace selection; it is a complete load-transfer problem extending from the MEP component to the building structure.

Integrated Seismic Support for HVAC and Mechanical Systems

HVAC systems often represent some of the most complex seismic-support coordination challenges because they combine heavy equipment, suspended ductwork, piping, rotating machinery, vibration isolation, and extensive overhead distribution.

Air-handling units, fans, pumps, boilers, chillers, rooftop equipment, and other mechanical components can require different support and restraint strategies based on their installation and operating characteristics.

HVAC Ductwork and Equipment

Suspended ductwork may require seismic bracing in multiple directions, with brace locations coordinated around joints, equipment connections, access panels, dampers, and other components. The supporting assembly may incorporate strut channels, threaded rods, brace members, clamps, and structural attachments.

Mechanical equipment presents additional considerations because its mass and center of gravity influence its response. Floor-mounted equipment may require engineered anchorage, while suspended equipment may require a combination of support and restraint.

Large HVAC systems also interact with connected piping and ductwork. During an earthquake, movement between equipment and surrounding systems can create demands at flexible connections and interfaces. A support strategy that addresses equipment anchorage while ignoring connected systems is therefore incomplete.

Vibration Isolation and Seismic Restraint

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint solve different engineering problems. Vibration isolators are intended to reduce transmission of operational vibration and structure-borne noise. Seismic restraints are intended to control movement and transfer applicable earthquake-induced forces.

A spring-isolated fan or pump, for example, may need to remain dynamically isolated during normal operation while also having an engineered means of resisting seismic movement. Seismic restraints must be coordinated so that they do not unnecessarily compromise the intended vibration-isolation performance.

This distinction is particularly important in hospitals, laboratories, data centers, and other facilities where both equipment performance and seismic protection matter. A coordinated approach may involve spring isolators, restraint assemblies, flexible connectors, and structural anchorage designed as parts of the same equipment-support strategy.

The engineering objective is to achieve the required combination of operational isolation and seismic restraint without treating either requirement as an afterthought.

Seismic Support for Piping, Plumbing, and Hydronic Systems

Piping systems introduce unique seismic-support challenges because they can extend across large portions of a building while carrying significant fluid weight. Hydronic piping, chilled-water systems, domestic water, fire protection, medical gas, and industrial process piping can all require project-specific evaluation.

Pipe Supports and Trapeze Assemblies

Pipe support systems commonly use hanger rods, clamps, trapeze frames, channels, brackets, and structural attachments. When seismic restraint is required, these components may be supplemented with dedicated brace assemblies capable of transferring forces into the structure.

A custom trapeze can be particularly useful where several pipes share a support location. Rather than installing separate supports for each line, an engineered trapeze can consolidate loads and create a coordinated support point. However, the resulting assembly still requires evaluation of member capacity, connection forces, anchor capacity, and the supporting structure.

Custom strut channels can also address unusual geometries, congested mechanical spaces, and project-specific attachment conditions. Fabricated brackets and frames can be designed around existing structural members or other MEP systems when standard configurations are impractical.

Thermal and Seismic Movement

Piping must also accommodate movement unrelated to earthquakes. Temperature changes can cause thermal expansion and contraction, while equipment operation can introduce vibration. Flexible connectors and expansion joints may be needed at appropriate interfaces.

Seismic restraint should therefore be coordinated with thermal movement rather than simply blocking every possible direction of motion. The designer must understand where movement is required and where restraint is necessary.

This becomes especially important near equipment, building joints, risers, and locations where piping crosses between structural areas. Poorly coordinated restraint can transfer unwanted loads into equipment nozzles or piping connections.

For healthcare facilities, fire protection and medical gas systems can introduce additional design and coordination requirements. The applicable code, project specifications, authority requirements, and system-specific standards must be evaluated rather than relying on a generic pipe-bracing detail.

Seismic Support for Electrical Conduit and Cable Trays

Electrical systems are critical components of modern buildings, and seismic support must address more than the weight of individual cables. Cable trays, conduit racks, electrical distribution equipment, and communication infrastructure can form extensive interconnected systems that require coordinated support.

Conduit Racks and Electrical Supports

Multiple conduits are often grouped on racks or trapeze-style assemblies. These supports may use strut channels, threaded rods, steel angles, channels, brackets, and custom frames. When seismic restraint is required, the assembly must provide an appropriate path for applicable lateral and longitudinal forces.

Electrical support design must also account for clearances, access, cable installation, maintenance, equipment interfaces, and coordination with other trades. A brace that satisfies a structural requirement but blocks a removable panel or conflicts with a neighboring duct is not a practical construction solution.

Cable Tray Seismic Bracing

Cable trays can span long distances and carry significant distributed loads. Their support configuration, connection details, and brace locations must be coordinated with the tray manufacturer, project requirements, and seismic design criteria.

An integrated approach considers the entire electrical support zone. Cable tray braces should not be designed without checking nearby piping, ductwork, conduit, lighting systems, and structural members.

In densely packed ceiling spaces, BIM 3D CAD coordination can help determine where seismic brace assemblies can physically fit. It can also identify potential conflicts before fabrication and installation.

Electrical systems in data centers, hospitals, transportation facilities, and industrial plants may have particularly demanding continuity requirements. These projects benefit from early coordination between structural engineers, electrical contractors, mechanical trades, and the support-system fabricator.

The result should be an engineered support strategy that addresses structural attachment, seismic restraint, physical coordination, and maintainability rather than treating cable tray and conduit support as isolated hardware selections.

Seismic Support for Healthcare and Critical Facilities

Healthcare and other critical facilities place special importance on coordinated seismic protection because the continued operation of MEP systems can directly affect building functionality after an earthquake. Hospitals depend on mechanical, electrical, plumbing, medical gas, emergency power, and other infrastructure that must be carefully coordinated with the building structure.

HCAI and OSHPD Considerations

California healthcare projects may fall under requirements administered by the California Department of Health Care Access and Information (HCAI), historically associated with the Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development (OSHPD). These projects can involve detailed structural and nonstructural seismic requirements, documentation, review, and inspection processes.

HCAI/OSHPD terminology should not be treated as a universal product certification. The applicable requirements depend on the facility, project, jurisdiction, scope, and governing provisions. Where a project calls for pre-approved or specifically documented components, the relevant project requirements should be verified during engineering and procurement.

Hospitals, Laboratories, and Mission-Critical Systems

Hospital MEP systems can include air-handling equipment, chilled-water piping, domestic water, medical gas, emergency generators, electrical distribution, cable trays, and communication infrastructure. Each system has different operational and structural considerations.

For example, an emergency generator may require equipment anchorage and structural evaluation, while connected exhaust, fuel, electrical, and ventilation systems require coordinated interfaces. Similarly, a suspended mechanical distribution system may need seismic bracing while also accommodating thermal and operational movement.

Mission-critical facilities such as data centers and central utility plants can present similar coordination challenges. Support systems must fit within congested structural and MEP zones while maintaining access to equipment and utilities.

An integrated engineering workflow helps coordinate these requirements from the beginning. Structural calculations, MEP layouts, BIM coordination, fabrication drawings, and installation details can be developed as interconnected deliverables rather than disconnected documents.

BIM 3D CAD Coordination for Integrated MEP Seismic Supports

BIM and 3D CAD modeling have become increasingly important for MEP seismic support because seismic brace assemblies occupy physical space that must be coordinated with the building's structure and other building systems.

MEP Clash Detection

A brace that works mathematically may not work physically. It may intersect a duct, pass through a cable tray, interfere with a pipe valve, obstruct equipment access, or encounter a structural member that cannot accept the intended attachment.

A coordinated 3D model allows engineers and contractors to evaluate these relationships before fabrication. Structural beams, slabs, ducts, pipes, cable trays, conduit, equipment, and seismic support assemblies can be reviewed within the same spatial environment.

Support Location and Fabrication Drawings

Once support locations are established, 3D CAD information can be developed into fabrication-ready geometry. Custom brackets, trapeze frames, channels, plates, gussets, and support assemblies can be detailed according to the engineered configuration.

This creates a direct connection between design intent and fabrication. Instead of relying on field modifications to resolve coordination problems, the support assembly can be developed around verified dimensions and attachment conditions.

Coordination Across Trades

Integrated BIM coordination is especially valuable in mechanical rooms, hospital utility spaces, data centers, and congested ceiling areas. The objective is not simply clash detection; it is coordinated constructability.

Custom Fabrication of Integrated MEP Seismic Support Systems

Standard support hardware is useful for many installations, but complex projects often require custom fabricated components. Structural conditions, limited attachment points, unusual equipment geometry, high MEP density, retrofit constraints, or multidisciplinary coordination can make custom fabrication more effective than forcing a standard assembly into an unsuitable location.

Strut Channels and Trapeze Assemblies

Custom strut-channel configurations can be developed for piping, conduit, cable trays, equipment supports, and combined MEP assemblies. Fabricated trapezes can consolidate multiple services while maintaining appropriate support and restraint geometry.

Steel plates, angles, channels, brackets, base plates, gusset plates, and reinforcement components can also be incorporated into custom assemblies where appropriate.

Steel Fabrication and Connection Components

Material selection depends on structural requirements, environmental exposure, corrosion conditions, temperature, project specifications, and fabrication requirements. Carbon steel and structural steel are common for many support applications, while stainless steel may be appropriate where corrosion resistance is important.

Fabrication processes can include laser cutting, plasma cutting, forming, machining, welding, stamping, galvanizing, and powder coating when suitable for the application. Welding procedures and inspection requirements should align with applicable project specifications and engineering requirements.

Custom fabrication does not replace engineering. Instead, fabrication translates an engineered support concept into a physical assembly. Dimensions, welds, bolt patterns, material thicknesses, attachment locations, and tolerances should be controlled through coordinated drawings and manufacturing documentation.

Engineering-to-Fabrication Workflow

A strong workflow connects:

Seismic criteria → engineering calculations → support design → structural attachment → BIM coordination → fabrication drawings → cutting/forming → welding/assembly → inspection → installation

This approach is particularly valuable when the same organization can coordinate engineering and fabrication. It reduces the disconnect that can occur when design assumptions are passed between unrelated parties and modified during manufacturing.

Seismic Retrofit and Existing MEP Systems

Seismic retrofit projects require a different approach from new construction because the engineer must work with existing structural and MEP conditions. Existing buildings may have undocumented modifications, limited attachment locations, congested mechanical spaces, or support configurations that were never designed for current seismic criteria.

Field Verification

Field verification is often essential before developing retrofit support details. Engineers and contractors may need to confirm structural member dimensions, slab conditions, existing anchors, pipe locations, equipment weights, support spacing, and available clearance.

A retrofit design based solely on outdated drawings can create significant installation problems. Actual field conditions should be reconciled with engineering assumptions before final fabrication.

Working Around Existing Conditions

Existing MEP systems may need to remain operational during construction. New braces may have to fit around occupied spaces, active utilities, insulation, valves, electrical infrastructure, or existing equipment.

Custom brackets, support frames, strut assemblies, and alternative structural attachments can provide flexibility where standard components cannot fit. However, each modification should be evaluated for its effect on the load path and surrounding structure.

Retrofit Seismic Upgrades

A retrofit may involve reinforcing existing supports, adding transverse or longitudinal restraint, anchoring equipment, replacing inadequate attachments, or redesigning portions of the MEP support system.

The process should begin with an assessment of the existing installation and applicable seismic criteria, followed by engineering calculations and coordinated drawings. BIM can still provide value in retrofit work by documenting existing conditions and visualizing new support geometry.

The combination of structural engineering, seismic calculations, field verification, CAD coordination, and custom fabrication is particularly useful when existing conditions make standardized retrofit solutions impractical.

Frequently Asked Questions About Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems

What is integrated seismic support for MEP systems?

Integrated seismic support is a coordinated engineering approach for protecting and restraining mechanical, electrical, and plumbing components against applicable earthquake-induced forces. It considers the MEP component, support assembly, brace, connection, anchor, and building structure as one load path. Rather than selecting seismic hardware independently for each trade, the approach coordinates HVAC, piping, plumbing, conduit, cable trays, and equipment with the structural system and with one another.

Why do MEP systems require seismic bracing and restraint?

During an earthquake, the building and its supported MEP components experience dynamic movement. Because MEP components have mass, inertial forces can develop at their supports and connections. Seismic bracing and restraint can be required to control movement and transfer applicable forces into the building structure. The exact requirements depend on the project, applicable seismic design criteria, component characteristics, and governing codes.

What is the difference between conventional MEP supports and seismic supports?

Conventional MEP supports primarily address gravity loads and service conditions. Seismic supports introduce additional requirements related to earthquake-induced forces and movement. A hanger, trapeze, bracket, or equipment base may carry weight but may not provide adequate lateral or longitudinal restraint. Seismic support design evaluates the complete load path, including brace members, connections, anchors, and structural attachments.

Which MEP systems typically require seismic support?

Potentially applicable systems include HVAC equipment and ductwork, piping, plumbing, fire protection, electrical conduit, cable trays, generators, pumps, chillers, boilers, air-handling equipment, and other nonstructural components. Requirements are not determined simply by the name of the system. Engineers must evaluate the project's seismic criteria, component characteristics, support arrangement, location, and applicable code provisions.

How does ASCE 7 apply to seismic support for MEP systems?

ASCE 7 contains provisions addressing seismic design of nonstructural components and their supports and attachments. It can establish or inform the seismic design parameters used to evaluate applicable MEP components. The specific provisions used depend on the adopted edition and project conditions. ASCE 7 should therefore be applied together with the governing building code, project criteria, and applicable engineering requirements rather than treated as a standalone generic bracing specification.

What are transverse and longitudinal seismic braces?

Transverse bracing generally provides restraint against movement across an elongated MEP system, while longitudinal bracing addresses movement along the system. Piping and ductwork can require restraint in multiple directions depending on their configuration and applicable design requirements. Brace spacing, locations, member capacity, and structural attachment must be determined through the appropriate engineering process.

How are MEP seismic supports attached to a building structure?

MEP supports can connect to structural concrete, structural steel, or other approved structural elements using engineered attachment methods. Depending on the application, these may include mechanical anchors, adhesive anchors, welded connections, bolted connections, base plates, beam connections, or fabricated structural attachments. Anchor and connection capacity must be evaluated as part of the complete load path. An adequately sized brace does not make an inadequately designed anchor acceptable.

Can vibration isolation and seismic restraint be used together?

Yes. Vibration isolation and seismic restraint address different performance objectives and can be coordinated in the same mechanical equipment installation. Spring isolators, rubber isolation components, or other vibration-control devices may reduce transmission of operating vibration, while seismic restraints address earthquake-induced movement. The restraint arrangement must be coordinated with the isolator's intended operating movement so that seismic protection does not unnecessarily defeat vibration-isolation performance.

What additional seismic considerations apply to hospitals and healthcare facilities?

Healthcare facilities can have stringent requirements because critical MEP systems support patient care and building functionality. California healthcare projects may also be subject to HCAI requirements, historically associated with OSHPD. HVAC, emergency power, medical gas, plumbing, electrical distribution, generators, and other systems may require detailed coordination and documentation. Applicable HCAI requirements should always be evaluated for the specific project rather than assumed from a generic healthcare application.

Can existing MEP systems be retrofitted with seismic supports?

Yes. Existing systems can often be evaluated for seismic retrofit, but retrofit design requires careful assessment of existing conditions. Field verification may be necessary to confirm structural members, concrete conditions, existing anchors, equipment weights, support spacing, and available attachment locations. Custom brackets, strut assemblies, support frames, and engineered anchorage can be developed where existing conditions prevent the use of standard configurations.

Can custom strut channels and fabricated support frames be used for MEP seismic bracing?

Custom strut channels, trapeze assemblies, brackets, frames, plates, and other fabricated components can be useful when project geometry or structural conditions do not fit standard support configurations. They should be designed and fabricated according to the applicable engineering requirements. Custom fabrication provides geometric flexibility, but it does not by itself establish seismic compliance. The engineered assembly, connections, anchorage, materials, fabrication quality, and installation must be evaluated as a complete system.

How does BIM improve integrated seismic support coordination?

BIM and 3D CAD can help identify conflicts between seismic braces and ducts, pipes, cable trays, conduit, equipment, architectural elements, and structural members before fabrication. This is especially useful in congested mechanical rooms and critical facilities. Coordinated models can also provide the dimensional information needed for fabrication drawings and custom support assemblies, creating a more direct connection between engineering design and field installation.

Conclusion

Integrated seismic support for MEP systems is fundamentally a load-path, coordination, and engineering problem, not simply a matter of adding braces to conventional supports. HVAC equipment, ductwork, piping, plumbing, conduit, cable trays, generators, and other MEP components interact with the building structure and with one another. Their seismic performance depends on how forces move from each component through its support and restraint system into the structural frame.

A reliable approach begins with project-specific seismic design criteria and continues through component evaluation, seismic force calculations, support configuration, brace selection, structural attachment, anchorage, and field verification. ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, and applicable HCAI requirements provide important frameworks, but the correct application depends on the project jurisdiction, building type, adopted code edition, seismic design criteria, and specific component conditions.

Integration is especially valuable where systems are dense or operationally critical. Hospitals, laboratories, data centers, industrial facilities, central utility plants, high-rise buildings, and retrofit projects often require coordination between structural systems and multiple MEP trades. BIM 3D CAD can identify conflicts before construction, while custom fabrication can translate engineered support geometry into practical brackets, trapezes, strut assemblies, and structural frames.

The distinction between vibration isolation and seismic restraint should also remain clear. Equipment may require both, but each addresses a different performance objective. Proper coordination allows vibration-control systems to function during normal operation while providing appropriate seismic restraint when required.
