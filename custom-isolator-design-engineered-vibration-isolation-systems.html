Custom Isolator Design: Engineered Vibration Isolation Systems

anitaa September 13, 2026

Modern mechanical and industrial equipment rarely operates under perfectly standard conditions. Pumps, fans, compressors, generators, engines, machine tools, HVAC equipment, and specialized machinery can differ significantly in weight, operating speed, mounting geometry, dynamic loading, environmental exposure, and allowable movement. When a standard vibration mount cannot satisfy those requirements, custom isolator design provides an engineering pathway for developing an isolation system around the actual equipment and its operating environment.

Custom isolation is more than changing the dimensions of an existing mount. A properly engineered system considers static load, dynamic load, center of gravity, mounting-point reactions, excitation frequency, stiffness, static deflection, natural frequency, damping, transmissibility, lateral movement, shock loading, fatigue, temperature, corrosion, and installation constraints. The objective is to control vibration transmission while maintaining the equipment's required stability, alignment, and operational performance.

The design process can involve spring isolators, wire rope isolators, rubber-metal mounts, elastomeric components, acoustic hangers, floor isolation systems, captive isolators, or combinations of these technologies. Material selection is equally important because rubber compounds, spring steel, stainless steel, carbon steel, aluminum, and protective coatings respond differently to temperature, chemicals, moisture, cyclic loading, and mechanical stress.

For commercial and industrial projects, custom isolation may also intersect with structural engineering and seismic protection. Vibration isolation and seismic restraint are not the same function, but equipment may require both. Applicable requirements under the IBC, CBC, ASCE 7, ACI 318, AISC standards, or HCAI requirements for qualifying healthcare projects may influence the structural support and anchorage portion of the installation.

The Sigma Source approaches custom isolator design as an integrated engineering and fabrication process. By connecting vibration-control analysis with BIM 3D CAD, structural coordination, custom metal fabrication, and project-specific support requirements, the design can move from equipment data to an engineered, coordinated, and constructible isolation solution.

What Is Custom Isolator Design?

Custom isolator design is the engineering development of a vibration isolation component or system specifically matched to the characteristics of a particular piece of equipment or installation. Rather than choosing a mount solely from a catalog based on equipment weight, the engineer evaluates how the equipment generates and transmits vibration and determines what isolation characteristics are needed.

Custom Vibration Isolation vs. Standard Mounts

Standard spring, rubber, and wire rope isolators can be highly effective when their load ranges, stiffness characteristics, dimensions, and operating conditions align with the application. Custom engineering becomes more valuable when the equipment falls outside those established conditions.

Examples include equipment with an unusual mounting footprint, uneven reactions at mounting points, unusual operating frequencies, restricted installation height, demanding environmental exposure, or specialized movement requirements. A custom system can also be useful when an OEM design must interface with a project-specific support structure.

The distinction is important because a physically compatible mount is not necessarily a dynamically appropriate mount. Two isolators may support the same nominal weight while producing very different stiffness, deflection, natural-frequency, and transmissibility characteristics.

Engineering Objectives

The fundamental objective is to reduce unwanted vibration transmission while maintaining adequate equipment support. Depending on the application, the design may also need to control lateral movement, accommodate thermal expansion, withstand shock, maintain alignment, or integrate with a structural base.

The resulting isolation system can include the isolator itself plus mounting plates, brackets, frames, inertia bases, restraints, or other supporting components. This makes custom isolation a system-level engineering task rather than simply a component-selection exercise.

How Engineers Develop a Custom Vibration Isolator

A successful custom vibration isolator begins with accurate equipment information. The engineer must understand what is being supported, how it operates, how it is mounted, and what environmental conditions the isolation system will experience.

Equipment Characterization

Important inputs can include equipment weight, center of gravity, mounting-point locations, equipment dimensions, operating speed, excitation frequencies, static and dynamic loads, startup and shutdown conditions, and expected movement. For rotating machinery, shaft speed and forcing frequencies can be particularly important. Reciprocating equipment may introduce more complex dynamic behavior.

The number and location of mounting points also matter. An equipment package with four mounts does not necessarily apply one-quarter of its total weight to every mount. Center-of-gravity location, frame stiffness, geometry, and operating loads can create unequal reactions.

Load Distribution

Engineers may determine the expected load at each mounting location before selecting an isolator. This helps prevent a common design problem: choosing an isolator based only on total equipment weight and overlooking actual point loads.

Load distribution becomes even more important for long equipment skids, engines, generators, compressors, and machinery with an elevated center of gravity.

Dynamic Requirements

Operating frequency is another fundamental input. The isolation system should be evaluated relative to the equipment's excitation frequency so that the isolator does not create an undesirable resonance condition.

The design may therefore require a specific spring rate, elastomer stiffness, damping characteristic, or multidirectional response. These requirements determine which isolation technology is appropriate.

Installation Constraints

Physical constraints can be just as important as dynamic requirements. Available height, footprint, mounting-hole patterns, structural attachment points, access for maintenance, equipment alignment, and surrounding piping or electrical connections can all influence the final geometry.

BIM 3D CAD modeling can help resolve these constraints before fabrication, particularly when equipment is installed in congested mechanical rooms, industrial plants, data centers, laboratories, or marine machinery spaces.

How Static Load, Stiffness, and Natural Frequency Affect Isolator Performance

Isolation performance depends on the relationship between equipment loading and the dynamic characteristics of the isolator. Three closely related considerations are static deflection, stiffness, and natural frequency.

Static Deflection

For spring-based isolation systems, static deflection is related to the supported load and spring rate. A softer spring generally produces greater deflection under the same load, while a stiffer spring produces less deflection.

This relationship is important because greater static deflection can correspond to a lower vertical natural frequency in an appropriately designed spring system. However, simply maximizing deflection is not a complete design strategy. Excessive movement can create alignment, clearance, or stability problems.

Natural Frequency

Natural frequency describes the frequency at which an isolation system tends to oscillate when disturbed. Effective isolation generally depends on creating an appropriate separation between the system's natural frequency and the equipment's dominant excitation frequency.

If the operating frequency is too close to the isolation system's natural frequency, vibration amplification can occur rather than the desired reduction in transmitted vibration.

Frequency Ratio and Transmissibility

The ratio between excitation frequency and natural frequency is an important parameter in evaluating transmissibility. As the operating frequency becomes sufficiently separated from the isolator's natural frequency, the system can enter a region where vibration transmission is reduced.

Damping also affects the response, particularly around resonance. A custom design therefore needs to consider stiffness and damping together rather than treating either characteristic independently.

Application-Specific Optimization

The correct isolation target depends on the equipment and installation. A large HVAC fan, precision machine, marine engine, and industrial compressor can require substantially different isolation characteristics.

The goal is not simply to specify the “softest” isolator. The objective is to develop an appropriate balance among isolation performance, movement, stability, durability, and structural compatibility.

Selecting the Right Custom Isolator Type

Different isolation technologies provide different combinations of stiffness, deflection, damping, movement control, shock response, and environmental durability. Selection should follow the equipment requirements rather than product category alone.

Custom Spring Isolators

Spring isolators are commonly considered for HVAC and mechanical equipment where substantial vertical deflection and controlled spring characteristics are required. They can be designed around equipment loads and mounting configurations, with restrained or captive arrangements considered when movement limits are necessary.

Spring systems can also be integrated with inertia bases, isolation frames, and structural supports.

Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed wire rope elements to provide multidirectional vibration and shock isolation. Their characteristics can make them useful for industrial, marine, transportation, electronics, and specialized equipment where shock loading, compact geometry, or environmental durability is important.

Custom wire rope isolator design can address specific load directions and mounting geometries rather than relying solely on a standard configuration.

Rubber-Metal and Elastomeric Isolators

Rubber-metal isolators and elastomeric mounts can provide compact solutions for equipment requiring controlled stiffness in one or more directions. The rubber compound, geometry, bonded interfaces, and loading conditions all influence performance.

These systems can be particularly useful where space is limited or where an application requires specific horizontal and vertical stiffness characteristics.

Acoustic and Floor Isolation

Acoustic hangers and floor isolation systems can address structure-borne vibration and noise transmission from suspended or floor-mounted equipment. These solutions are often relevant in mechanical rooms, healthcare facilities, laboratories, commercial buildings, and sensitive equipment environments.

Captive and Restrained Systems

Captive isolators, restraints, snubbers, and limit stops can be incorporated where equipment movement must remain within defined limits. If seismic protection is required, restraint and anchorage should be engineered as separate but coordinated elements of the overall equipment-support system.

Materials for Custom Vibration Isolator Design

Material selection directly affects isolator behavior, durability, and suitability for the operating environment. The strongest material in a conventional structural sense is not automatically the best material for vibration isolation.

Elastomer Selection

Natural rubber, neoprene, EPDM, silicone, nitrile, polyurethane, and other elastomeric compounds can provide different combinations of stiffness, damping, temperature resistance, chemical compatibility, and durability.

For example, an isolator exposed to oils or fuels may require a different elastomer than one operating in a general indoor mechanical room. Marine environments may introduce saltwater and corrosion considerations, while industrial equipment can expose isolation components to heat, chemicals, or repeated dynamic loading.

Metal Components

Steel components may include springs, mounting plates, brackets, frames, housings, base plates, and structural supports. Stainless steel can be useful where corrosion resistance is important, while carbon or structural steel can be appropriate for many industrial and commercial applications.

Spring steel and alloy steel may be selected for components where specific mechanical characteristics are required.

Corrosion Protection

Galvanizing, plating, powder coating, and other protective treatments can extend the service life of metal components in appropriate environments. The coating should be compatible with the operating environment and fabrication requirements.

Material Compatibility

Material selection should consider the entire system. Temperature, chemical exposure, moisture, fatigue, compression, shear, tension, shock, and long-term environmental conditions can all influence material performance.

For custom systems, these factors should be incorporated during engineering rather than addressed after the geometry has already been finalized.

Custom Isolator Design for HVAC and Mechanical Equipment

HVAC and mechanical equipment are among the most common applications where vibration isolation and structural support must be coordinated. Fans, pumps, compressors, chillers, air-handling units, cooling equipment, and mechanical skids can transmit operational vibration into floors, roofs, structural frames, and connected building systems.

HVAC Equipment

Equipment weight alone does not determine the correct isolator. Operating speed, rotating components, equipment frame stiffness, center of gravity, mounting locations, and required movement all influence the design.

For example, a fan with a relatively high operating speed can require different dynamic isolation characteristics from a slow-speed pump, even if their supported weights are similar.

Equipment Support

Custom isolator systems may incorporate floor mounts, spring isolators, isolation rails, inertia bases, equipment frames, isolation curbs, or other support structures.

An inertia base can increase mass and improve the dynamic characteristics of certain equipment installations, but its design must be coordinated with the isolators and supporting structure.

Connected MEP Systems

Isolation movement must also be compatible with connected piping, ductwork, electrical connections, and flexible connectors. Rigidly connecting an isolated piece of equipment to surrounding systems can create unintended force paths that reduce the effectiveness of the isolation system.

Mechanical equipment may therefore require flexible connections at appropriate interfaces, with sufficient allowance for expected movement.

Seismic Coordination

Vibration isolation does not automatically provide seismic protection. Where earthquake restraint is required, seismic restraints, structural attachments, and anchorage should be engineered around the isolated equipment.

For applicable projects, the structural and seismic portion of the design may need to consider ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, ACI 318, or project-specific requirements. The isolation system and seismic restraint should be coordinated so that seismic protection does not unnecessarily compromise normal vibration-isolation movement.

Custom Isolator Design for Industrial, Marine, and Specialized Equipment

Industrial and specialized machinery often creates more demanding isolation requirements because operating conditions can involve high dynamic forces, shock, cyclic loading, corrosion, unusual mounting geometry, or continuous operation.

Industrial Machinery

Motors, compressors, generators, pumps, machine tools, process equipment, production machinery, and equipment skids can all benefit from equipment-specific isolation analysis.

Industrial machinery may generate both fundamental operating-frequency vibration and harmonics. A custom isolator must therefore be evaluated against the relevant excitation characteristics rather than a single nominal operating speed.

Marine Engine Mounts

Marine engines and propulsion equipment can impose vibration, shock, thermal, and corrosion demands on mounting systems. Limited machinery-space geometry can also require specialized mounting arrangements.

Custom marine engine mounts may incorporate elastomeric or wire rope technologies depending on the required load directions, stiffness, shock characteristics, environmental conditions, and installation geometry.

Aerospace and Precision Equipment

Precision machinery and sensitive equipment may have tighter vibration requirements than conventional mechanical equipment. Small vibration levels can affect measurement accuracy, optical systems, manufacturing processes, or sensitive instrumentation.

In such applications, equipment sensitivity, excitation spectrum, mounting geometry, and structural response should all be considered.

Harsh Operating Environments

Industrial and marine applications can expose isolators to temperature extremes, moisture, chemicals, oil, fuel, saltwater, dust, and cyclic mechanical loading.

The design must therefore integrate material selection with dynamic performance. A material that provides appropriate stiffness but deteriorates under the operating environment is not a suitable long-term isolation solution.

Custom Isolator Engineering for Shock, Impact, and Multidirectional Loads

Not all vibration problems are steady-state operating problems. Some equipment experiences startup transients, shutdown events, impacts, shock loads, or significant movement in multiple directions.

Shock Isolation

Wire rope and specialized elastomeric systems can be considered for applications where shock and transient loading are important. The engineering objective is to control the transfer of dynamic energy while keeping equipment movement within acceptable limits.

The required response depends on shock magnitude, duration, direction, equipment mass, mounting geometry, and allowable displacement.

Horizontal and Lateral Stiffness

An isolator can have different stiffness characteristics in vertical, horizontal, axial, or shear directions. Custom geometry allows engineers to address these directional requirements more precisely.

This is important for equipment subjected to lateral forces, acceleration, or operational loads that are not purely vertical.

Overturning and Restraint

Equipment with a high center of gravity may have an increased tendency to rotate or overturn under lateral loading. The isolation system should therefore be evaluated for stability and allowable movement.

Captive mounts, limit stops, snubbers, and other restraint components can be incorporated where appropriate. These elements should be engineered around actual equipment movement rather than added without evaluating their effect on normal isolation.

Fatigue and Cyclic Loading

Industrial and marine equipment can operate continuously for long periods, exposing isolators to millions of dynamic cycles. Fatigue considerations may influence material selection, geometry, stress levels, attachment details, and inspection requirements.

Custom isolator engineering should therefore consider service life and operating cycles when those factors are significant to the application.

BIM 3D CAD and Custom Fabrication for Isolation Systems

A custom isolator must ultimately fit into a real mechanical and structural environment. BIM 3D CAD provides a practical way to connect dynamic engineering requirements with equipment geometry, support conditions, and fabrication constraints.

3D Equipment Modeling

CAD models can capture equipment dimensions, mounting points, base patterns, isolation components, brackets, frames, and structural interfaces. This provides a visual and dimensional basis for evaluating the proposed system.

Clash Detection

Isolation systems can occupy valuable space beneath or around equipment. A mounting bracket may interfere with piping, a frame may conflict with a structural beam, or an isolator may require more vertical clearance than the mechanical room allows.

Three-dimensional coordination can identify these conflicts before fabrication, reducing the need for field modifications.

Custom Fabrication

Once the engineered geometry is established, fabrication may involve custom base plates, mounting brackets, isolation frames, structural frames, equipment supports, custom strut channels, welded assemblies, and related steel components.

The Sigma Source's metal fabrication capabilities can support processes such as laser cutting, plasma cutting, forming, machining, welding, galvanizing, and powder coating where appropriate to the component and project requirements.

Engineering-to-Fabrication Workflow

A coordinated workflow can progress from:

Equipment data → vibration analysis → custom isolator design → engineering calculations → 3D CAD → fabrication drawings → prototype or production fabrication → inspection/testing → installation

This approach maintains a connection between the calculated design requirements and the physical assembly. Fabrication should implement the engineered design rather than substitute for engineering analysis.

Custom Isolators and Seismic Restraint: What Is the Difference?

Vibration isolation and seismic protection are frequently discussed together on mechanical projects, but they address fundamentally different engineering objectives.

Vibration Isolation

An isolator is primarily intended to reduce the transmission of operational vibration from equipment into its supporting structure or from surrounding structures into sensitive equipment.

Spring isolators, rubber-metal mounts, elastomeric systems, wire rope isolators, and floor isolation systems can accomplish this through controlled stiffness, deflection, damping, or other dynamic characteristics.

Seismic Restraint

Seismic restraints are intended to control equipment movement and transfer applicable earthquake-induced forces into the supporting structure. They can include braces, snubbers, structural frames, anchors, and other restraint components.

A seismic restraint should not automatically be described as a vibration isolator, and an isolator should not automatically be assumed to provide seismic restraint.

Structural Anchorage

The supporting structure is part of the load path. Equipment loads and applicable seismic forces may pass through an isolator, mounting assembly, base plate, anchor rods or anchors, and supporting concrete or structural steel.

Where concrete anchorage is involved, applicable ACI 318 provisions may become relevant. Structural steel support and attachment design may involve applicable AISC requirements.

Integrated Equipment Protection

Some equipment requires both vibration isolation and seismic restraint. Hospitals, laboratories, commercial buildings, data centers, industrial plants, and other facilities may require coordinated treatment of these requirements.

For applicable California projects, the CBC and project-specific HCAI requirements may also influence the engineering and documentation process. The exact requirements depend on the facility, jurisdiction, adopted code edition, equipment, and project scope.

The correct objective is therefore not to make the isolation system unnecessarily rigid. It is to coordinate isolation, restraint, anchorage, and structural support so each performs its intended function.

Testing, Inspection, and Performance Verification of Custom Isolators

Engineering calculations establish the design basis, but verification can be important when a custom isolation system has specialized performance requirements or when project specifications require documented testing.

Engineering Verification

The design should be reviewed against the required load range, stiffness, deflection, geometry, expected operating conditions, and movement limits. For dynamic systems, predicted natural frequency and transmissibility characteristics can be evaluated against the intended equipment operating conditions.

Prototype Evaluation

A prototype can be useful when a new geometry, unusual material combination, or specialized application needs to be evaluated before full production.

Prototype work can identify manufacturing or installation issues early and provide an opportunity to compare measured performance with engineering expectations.

Material and Fabrication Inspection

Inspection may include dimensional verification, material documentation, weld inspection where applicable, coating checks, fastener verification, and assembly review.

For fabricated steel components, inspection requirements should correspond to project specifications and engineering documentation.

Performance Testing

Depending on the application, dynamic testing or other performance verification may be appropriate. Testing requirements should be established according to the equipment, isolation objectives, project specifications, and risk profile.

Not every custom isolator requires the same testing program. A straightforward equipment mount and a highly specialized industrial or precision isolation system may have very different verification requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions About Custom Isolator Design

What is custom isolator design?

Custom isolator design is the engineering process of developing a vibration isolation system around the specific requirements of equipment. Instead of selecting a standard mount solely from its rated load, engineers consider equipment weight, load distribution, center of gravity, operating speed, excitation frequency, stiffness, natural frequency, damping, mounting geometry, environmental exposure, movement, and structural support conditions. The resulting design may use springs, wire rope, elastomers, rubber-metal components, or a combination of isolation and support elements.

When is a custom vibration isolator necessary?

A custom isolator may be appropriate when standard products cannot provide the required load capacity, stiffness, deflection, geometry, environmental durability, or dynamic performance. It can also be justified when equipment has unusual mounting points, operates at a specialized frequency, experiences shock or multidirectional loads, or must fit within a restricted installation space. The decision should be based on engineering requirements rather than assuming that every specialized piece of equipment needs a completely custom isolator.

How are custom vibration isolators designed?

The process normally begins with equipment characterization. Engineers evaluate weight, center of gravity, mounting locations, operating speed, excitation characteristics, static and dynamic loads, dimensions, environmental conditions, and installation constraints. They can then determine appropriate stiffness, static deflection, natural frequency, damping, movement limits, and material characteristics. The selected isolation technology is subsequently developed into a physical geometry that can be coordinated through CAD and fabricated according to the engineering requirements.

What information is needed to design a custom isolator?

Useful information includes equipment weight, center of gravity, number and location of mounting points, mounting-hole pattern, equipment dimensions, operating speed, excitation frequencies, startup and shutdown conditions, static and dynamic loads, allowable movement, temperature, chemical exposure, moisture, and available installation space. Drawings, OEM documentation, equipment specifications, and existing support information can also help establish the design basis. Accurate information is particularly important when load distribution varies between mounting points.

Can custom isolators be designed for HVAC equipment?

Yes. HVAC equipment such as air-handling units, chillers, pumps, fans, compressors, cooling equipment, and mechanical skids can require equipment-specific vibration isolation. The design can consider equipment weight, operating speed, rotating components, support geometry, inertia bases, isolation curbs, floor conditions, and connected piping or ductwork. Where seismic restraint is also required, the isolation and restraint systems should be coordinated rather than treating seismic protection as an automatic function of the vibration isolator.

What materials can be used for custom isolators?

Depending on the application, custom isolation systems can use natural rubber, neoprene, EPDM, silicone, nitrile, polyurethane, other elastomeric compounds, spring steel, carbon steel, stainless steel, alloy steel, aluminum, wire rope, and composite materials. Selection should account for stiffness, damping, temperature, chemical exposure, corrosion, fatigue, shock, and service conditions. Protective treatments such as galvanizing, plating, or powder coating may be appropriate for metal components when environmental exposure requires additional protection.

Can a custom isolator also provide seismic protection?

A vibration isolator and a seismic restraint perform different functions. An isolator is primarily designed to reduce operational vibration transmission, while seismic restraint is intended to control earthquake-induced movement and transfer applicable forces to the structure. However, both can be incorporated into a coordinated equipment-support system. The design may include restrained springs, captive isolators, snubbers, structural frames, anchors, or other restraint components where project requirements call for them.

Are custom isolators suitable for marine and industrial applications?

Yes. Marine engines, propulsion machinery, generators, pumps, compressors, process equipment, and industrial machinery can have demanding isolation requirements involving shock, vibration, corrosion, temperature, cyclic loading, and limited installation space. Wire rope and elastomeric technologies are among the options that may be considered depending on the load directions and environmental conditions. Custom engineering is particularly useful when standard mounts cannot accommodate the equipment geometry or operating environment.

How does BIM 3D CAD support custom isolator design?

BIM 3D CAD allows the equipment, isolators, mounting plates, frames, structural members, piping, ductwork, electrical connections, and surrounding construction to be reviewed within a coordinated spatial model. This can reveal clearance and clash issues before fabrication. CAD information can also support fabrication drawings for custom brackets, base plates, isolation frames, and structural assemblies. For retrofit projects, three-dimensional modeling can help document existing conditions and coordinate new isolation hardware around existing infrastructure.

How are custom isolators tested?

Testing depends on the application and project requirements. Engineering verification may address load, stiffness, deflection, geometry, natural frequency, and expected dynamic performance. A prototype may be developed when a specialized design warrants physical evaluation before production. Fabricated components can also undergo dimensional, material, weld, coating, or assembly inspection as applicable. Dynamic performance testing may be appropriate for specialized systems, but it should be specified according to the equipment, isolation objectives, and required verification level rather than assumed to be identical for every application.

What standards apply to custom isolator design?

There is no single building code that universally defines the design of every vibration isolator. The applicable standards depend on the equipment, project, structural support, seismic requirements, materials, and jurisdiction. ASCE 7, IBC, and CBC may be relevant when seismic forces and structural support are involved. ACI 318 can apply to concrete anchorage, while AISC standards may apply to structural steel supports and attachments. AWS and ASTM requirements may become relevant to fabrication and materials. California healthcare projects may also involve HCAI requirements historically associated with OSHPD. These standards should be evaluated according to the actual project rather than treated as blanket certification of a custom isolator.

Conclusion

Custom isolator design provides an engineering solution when standard vibration mounts cannot adequately address the physical, dynamic, environmental, or installation requirements of a particular equipment application. The most effective design process begins with the equipment itself: its weight, center of gravity, mounting configuration, operating speed, excitation characteristics, static and dynamic loads, allowable movement, and operating environment.

From there, engineers can evaluate the relationships among stiffness, static deflection, natural frequency, damping, frequency ratio, and transmissibility. Those characteristics help determine whether spring isolators, wire rope isolators, rubber-metal mounts, elastomeric systems, acoustic hangers, floor isolation, captive configurations, or another approach is appropriate.

Material selection is equally important. Rubber compounds and elastomers must be matched to temperature, chemical exposure, moisture, fatigue, and required stiffness, while steel, stainless steel, spring steel, aluminum, wire rope, and protective coatings must be selected according to structural, mechanical, environmental, and fabrication requirements.

Custom isolation also needs to function within the real building or industrial environment. BIM 3D CAD can coordinate equipment geometry, mounting points, structural members, piping, ductwork, electrical systems, access requirements, and custom support assemblies before fabrication. Custom metal fabrication can then translate the engineered design into base plates, brackets, isolation frames, structural supports, and other components through appropriate cutting, forming, welding, machining, galvanizing, or coating processes.

Where seismic requirements apply, vibration isolation should remain distinct from seismic restraint. Equipment may need both operational vibration control and earthquake protection, requiring coordinated restraints, structural attachments, and anchorage. Applicable requirements can involve ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, ACI 318, AISC standards, and project-specific HCAI requirements for qualifying healthcare facilities.

The Sigma Source brings these disciplines together through vibration-control engineering, seismic calculations, structural engineering, BIM 3D CAD modeling, and custom metal fabrication. This integrated approach allows custom isolation systems to be developed around actual equipment requirements and then coordinated through engineering, fabrication, verification, and installation. The result is not simply a customized mount, but an engineered isolation solution designed to function within the complete mechanical, structural, and operational environment.
