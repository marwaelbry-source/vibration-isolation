Vibration Isolation: Systems, Design Principles, and Applications

anitaa September 13, 2026

Modern buildings and industrial facilities increasingly rely on mechanical and rotating equipment that can generate significant dynamic forces during operation. Fans, pumps, chillers, compressors, motors, air handling units, generators, and manufacturing machinery can transmit vibration through their mounting points and into structural systems. When that energy reaches occupied spaces, sensitive equipment, piping, ducts, or architectural finishes, the result may include objectionable vibration, structure-borne noise, equipment movement, fatigue, or operational problems.

vibration isolation provides an engineered method for reducing the transmission of these dynamic forces between equipment and its supporting structure. Rather than treating vibration as simply a noise problem, effective isolation considers the mechanical characteristics of the equipment, operating speed, excitation frequency, supported weight, isolator stiffness, damping, static deflection, structural conditions, and system connections.

For U.S. construction projects, vibration isolation may also need to be coordinated with seismic restraint, equipment anchorage, mechanical design, and applicable building-code requirements. This is particularly important in California projects and healthcare facilities where structural, mechanical, and seismic requirements can intersect.

The appropriate solution is therefore rarely determined by isolator type alone. A spring isolator, wire rope isolator, elastomeric mount, acoustic hanger, or captive isolation system must be evaluated in relation to the equipment and its installation environment.

For engineers, contractors, architects, facility managers, and specification teams, understanding the fundamentals of vibration isolation makes it easier to identify the right technology, avoid common installation problems, and coordinate vibration control with structural and seismic requirements. The following guide examines the engineering principles, isolation technologies, applications, selection criteria, and project considerations that influence a properly designed vibration isolation system.

What Is Vibration Isolation and How Does It Work?

Vibration isolation is the controlled reduction of dynamic force transmission between a vibrating source and the structure supporting it. In a typical mechanical installation, vibration can follow a path from the equipment through its mounting hardware, base, housekeeping pad or structural support, and ultimately into the building structure.

An isolation system introduces a compliant mechanical interface into that path. Instead of allowing equipment forces to transfer directly into the supporting structure, the isolator provides controlled stiffness and, depending on the design, damping and other response characteristics.

Vibration Transmission in Mechanical Systems

Consider a centrifugal fan mounted directly to a structural frame. During operation, rotating components generate dynamic forces. If the equipment is rigidly connected to the frame, much of that excitation can be transmitted directly into the structure.

Installing an appropriately selected vibration isolation mount changes the mechanical relationship between the equipment and support. The mount can reduce transmitted forces when the operating conditions are suitable for the isolator's characteristics.

The complete system still matters. Rigid piping connections, ductwork, conduit, electrical connections, structural members, or improperly installed anchors can create vibration bypass paths around the isolators. Consequently, isolation cannot be evaluated solely by looking at the mount.

Isolation Versus Vibration Absorption

Isolation and damping are related but different engineering concepts. Isolation seeks to reduce force or motion transmission across an interface, while damping dissipates vibratory energy and influences system response, particularly near resonance.

A successful design considers both where appropriate. The objective is not simply to make an equipment mount soft or flexible. The isolator must have characteristics compatible with equipment loading, excitation frequency, allowable movement, environmental conditions, and structural requirements.

Why Vibration Isolation Matters in Building and Industrial Systems

Mechanical vibration becomes an engineering concern when its magnitude, frequency, or transmission path interferes with equipment operation, building performance, occupant comfort, acoustic conditions, or sensitive processes. A vibration level that is acceptable for one industrial machine may be unacceptable in a hospital imaging environment, laboratory, precision manufacturing area, or occupied office located next to a mechanical room.

Mechanical and Structural Vibration

Rotating machinery can generate excitation from imbalance, shaft conditions, bearing behavior, coupling issues, aerodynamic forces, or other operating characteristics. Reciprocating machinery can introduce more complex periodic forces. Once these forces reach a structural support, the building itself can respond dynamically.

Structural characteristics therefore influence the final result. Floor construction, support stiffness, spans, equipment location, and connection details can all affect vibration transmission.

Vibration and Noise Control

Vibration isolation can contribute to acoustic control by reducing structure-borne excitation, but it should not automatically be treated as a complete noise-control solution. Airborne noise, duct-borne noise, equipment-generated sound, and architectural acoustics may require separate measures.

For example, an HVAC fan can transmit vibration through its base while simultaneously producing airborne sound. Isolators may address part of the structural transmission path, while acoustic enclosures, duct treatments, flexible connections, or other measures may be needed for the broader noise-control strategy.

This distinction is particularly important when developing specifications. The desired performance should be defined first, followed by selection of the appropriate isolation and supporting systems.

Key Engineering Principles of Vibration Isolation

The performance of a vibration isolation system depends on the relationship between the equipment's excitation and the dynamic characteristics of the isolation system. Several engineering parameters are fundamental to understanding that relationship.

Natural Frequency

An isolated equipment system has a natural frequency determined largely by its effective stiffness and supported mass. For a simplified single-degree-of-freedom system, natural frequency is related to the ratio of stiffness to mass.

Isolation generally becomes more effective when the excitation frequency is sufficiently higher than the isolation system's natural frequency. This is why simply selecting a mount with a high load rating does not guarantee effective vibration isolation.

Excitation Frequency

Equipment operating speed is an important input. A motor operating at a particular RPM generates a fundamental rotational frequency, while fans, pumps, compressors, and other equipment may produce additional harmonics or excitation frequencies.

The isolation system should therefore be evaluated against the equipment's actual operating range rather than only its nameplate weight.

Transmissibility

Transmissibility describes the relationship between the response transmitted through an isolation system and the excitation applied to it. Near resonance, transmissibility can increase substantially. At sufficiently higher excitation-to-natural-frequency ratios, isolation performance generally improves.

This makes frequency separation a central consideration in vibration isolation design.

Static Deflection

Static deflection is the amount an isolator deflects under the supported load. For spring isolators, static deflection is closely related to stiffness and natural frequency.

Load must be evaluated at each support point rather than simply dividing the total equipment weight equally when the actual center of gravity, support geometry, or equipment configuration produces unequal reactions.

Damping and Resonance

Damping affects the amplitude of response around resonance. Higher damping can reduce resonant amplification, although damping characteristics also influence isolation performance in the isolation region.

A technically appropriate system balances stiffness and damping with the equipment's operating characteristics rather than maximizing either parameter independently.

Types of Vibration Isolation Systems

Different equipment and operating environments call for different isolation technologies. The selection should be based on loading, frequency, required movement, environmental conditions, durability, space limitations, and integration with the surrounding structure.

Spring Vibration Isolators

Spring isolators are commonly used where relatively high static deflection and low natural frequency are desirable. They can be appropriate for HVAC equipment, fans, pumps, air handling equipment, chillers, and other mechanical systems.

Restrained or housed configurations can provide additional control of movement where equipment must also interact with seismic restraint requirements. The restraint arrangement must be engineered so that it does not unintentionally compromise the intended isolation behavior.

Wire Rope Vibration Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed wire rope elements to provide compliance in multiple directions. Their construction can make them useful for equipment exposed to vibration, shock, movement, and demanding environmental conditions.

Unlike a simple linear spring model, wire rope behavior can be nonlinear and direction-dependent. This makes load, deflection, geometry, and application conditions important during selection.

Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Elastomeric mounts use rubber or synthetic elastomer compounds to provide controlled stiffness and damping. Rubber-in-shear configurations are widely used for compact equipment mounting where moderate isolation requirements and limited installation space influence the design.

Material characteristics can vary with temperature, frequency, aging, and environmental exposure, so the specific compound and operating environment should be considered.

Rubber/Metal Isolators

Rubber/metal isolators combine elastomeric elements with metal components that provide mounting interfaces and structural load paths. They can offer a compact solution for machinery and equipment where space, attachment geometry, and directional stiffness are important.

Acoustic Hangers and Ceiling Isolation

Suspended HVAC and MEP systems can transmit vibration through hanger rods and structural attachments. Acoustic hangers and ceiling isolation components introduce an isolation interface into suspended systems, helping reduce vibration transmission through the building structure.

Floor and Captive Vibration Isolators

Floor isolation systems support equipment at structural or housekeeping-pad level. Captive isolators can provide controlled movement while maintaining a defined mechanical relationship between equipment and support.

The choice between these systems depends on equipment loading, required movement, operating conditions, seismic considerations, and the available installation geometry.

How to Select the Right Vibration Isolator

Selecting a vibration isolation mount should begin with the equipment and operating conditions rather than with a preferred product category. A technically sound selection considers the complete dynamic system.

Equipment Weight and Load Distribution

Obtain the operating weight, not just shipping weight. Equipment may have different operating conditions depending on fluid levels, accessories, internal components, or connected systems.

The center of gravity and actual mounting-point reactions should also be evaluated. Unequal loading can cause one isolator to operate outside its intended range if all mounts are selected using only an average load.

Operating Speed and Excitation Frequency

RPM should be converted into frequency and evaluated against the isolation system's natural frequency. Where multiple excitation sources exist, the analysis should consider relevant harmonics and operating ranges.

Variable-speed equipment deserves particular attention because the operating frequency can pass through resonant regions during startup or speed changes.

Required Static Deflection

Static deflection provides an important indication of isolator stiffness. Higher deflection systems may be appropriate where lower natural frequency is required, but they also generally involve greater movement and therefore require appropriate clearance and connection detailing.

Environmental Conditions

Temperature, moisture, corrosion, chemicals, ultraviolet exposure, marine conditions, and shock loading can influence material and hardware selection. Galvanized or stainless-steel components may be considered where corrosion resistance is important.

Space and Mounting Constraints

Equipment footprint, mounting holes, base dimensions, support locations, overhead clearance, and available movement envelope can determine which isolation technology is practical.

The final selection should account for installation as well as theoretical performance. An isolator that performs well on paper may be unsuitable if it cannot be installed or maintained within the available space.

Vibration Isolation for HVAC and MEP Equipment

HVAC and MEP systems are among the most common applications for vibration isolation because mechanical equipment frequently contains rotating components and is directly connected to building systems.

Air Handling Units and Fans

Fans can produce vibration from imbalance, aerodynamic effects, bearing conditions, or drive components. Air handling units may also transmit vibration through their supporting frames and connections.

Spring isolators, elastomeric mounts, or other isolation configurations may be selected depending on equipment size, operating frequency, support conditions, and required movement.

Pumps, Chillers, and Compressors

Pumps and compressors can generate significant dynamic forces that require consideration of equipment mounting, piping connections, and structural support.

Chillers and other large equipment may require coordinated isolation bases or inertia bases where equipment mass and support geometry influence system behavior.

Rooftop Mechanical Equipment

Rooftop installations introduce additional concerns because the equipment is supported directly by the building's roof structure and is exposed to weather. Isolation design must be coordinated with structural support, equipment anchorage, access requirements, and applicable wind and seismic considerations.

Flexible Connections and System Integration

An isolated piece of equipment can lose much of its intended isolation performance if connected systems create rigid bypass paths. Flexible connectors may therefore be necessary at piping or ductwork interfaces, subject to the specific mechanical design.

Electrical conduit and other services must also be coordinated so they do not impose unintended rigid restraints on equipment intended to move on its isolators.

Vibration Isolation for Industrial and Rotating Equipment

Industrial machinery can generate substantially different vibration characteristics from conventional building HVAC equipment. Machine speed, load cycles, dynamic forces, process conditions, and foundation stiffness all influence the isolation strategy.

Motors and Rotating Machinery

Imbalance, misalignment, bearing conditions, coupling behavior, and operating speed can contribute to vibration. Isolation should not be used to conceal an underlying mechanical fault. If a machine is poorly aligned or improperly balanced, correcting the source may be more effective than simply modifying the mounting system.

Compressors and Reciprocating Equipment

Reciprocating machinery can generate periodic forces in multiple directions. The isolation system may need to account for vertical and horizontal dynamic loads as well as static equipment weight.

Manufacturing and Aerospace Facilities

Manufacturing and aerospace environments may contain equipment with tight vibration tolerances or processes affected by structural movement. Machine tools, test equipment, production machinery, and precision instruments can require carefully controlled support conditions.

Marine Engine Mounts

Marine equipment presents additional challenges involving shock, vibration, moisture, corrosion, and dynamic loading. Marine engine mounts may therefore require specialized materials, geometry, and directional stiffness characteristics.

For these applications, the isolation system should be evaluated against the actual operational and environmental conditions rather than selected solely from static load capacity.

Vibration Isolation and Seismic Restraint: What Is the Difference?

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint serve different engineering purposes, although they can occur in the same installation. Understanding this distinction is particularly important in U.S. commercial construction and California projects.

Vibration Isolation

Vibration isolation primarily addresses dynamic vibration and force transmission during equipment operation. Its purpose is to reduce the transfer of mechanical excitation between equipment and supporting structures.

The isolator must remain sufficiently compliant to achieve the intended dynamic behavior.

Seismic Restraint

Seismic restraint addresses equipment movement and forces associated with earthquake loading. Anchors, braces, snubbers, restraints, and other components may be incorporated into the system depending on the equipment and project requirements.

A seismic restraint should not automatically be treated as a vibration isolator, and a vibration isolator should not automatically be assumed to satisfy seismic restraint requirements.

When Both Systems Are Required

Mechanical equipment can require both isolation and seismic restraint. This creates a design challenge because restraints must accommodate the intended isolation movement while still providing appropriate resistance under seismic loading.

For California projects, the design team may need to coordinate structural calculations, mechanical equipment information, anchorage, restraint geometry, and applicable requirements associated with the California Building Code and HCAI projects.

The engineering objective is an integrated system rather than two independent components installed without coordination.

Vibration Isolation in Healthcare, Laboratories, and Sensitive Facilities

Sensitive facilities often require greater attention to vibration because equipment performance, patient environments, research activities, or precision processes can be affected by structural movement.

Hospitals and Healthcare Facilities

Hospitals contain a wide range of mechanical equipment, patient-care spaces, laboratories, imaging systems, and other sensitive functions. Mechanical-room vibration can propagate through structural elements and connected building services.

Healthcare projects can also involve additional review and documentation requirements. HCAI projects, historically associated with OSHPD terminology, require careful coordination of structural and mechanical design requirements.

Laboratories and Precision Equipment

Laboratories may contain microscopes, analytical instruments, measurement equipment, and other systems sensitive to floor vibration. The appropriate solution depends on the instrument's vibration criteria, location, excitation sources, and structural conditions.

Isolation should therefore be coordinated with the facility's actual performance criteria rather than assumed to provide a universal level of protection.

Data Centers and Critical Facilities

Data centers and critical facilities contain extensive mechanical infrastructure, including fans, pumps, cooling systems, and other rotating equipment. Vibration control must be integrated with structural support, equipment reliability, maintenance access, and seismic requirements.

In all sensitive environments, the isolation strategy should begin with defined performance requirements and a clear understanding of the vibration source and transmission path.

Installation and System Integration Considerations

Even a properly selected vibration isolation system can perform poorly when installation details are incorrect. Field conditions determine whether the design intent is preserved after equipment is installed.

Mounting and Alignment

Equipment should be properly aligned and supported at the locations assumed during isolator selection. Uneven loading can change isolator deflection and introduce unwanted equipment tilt.

Mounting hardware should also be compatible with the equipment base and designed for the expected loads.

Equipment Base and Inertia Bases

Some installations use structural bases or inertia bases between the equipment and isolators. Increasing system mass can influence dynamic behavior and may help address equipment stability, depending on the application.

The base, isolators, and equipment should be evaluated as a complete assembly.

Piping, Ductwork, and Connected Services

Connected systems must accommodate the expected movement of isolated equipment. Rigid connections can effectively bridge the isolation interface and transmit vibration around the mount.

Coordination between mechanical, electrical, structural, and architectural disciplines is therefore essential.

Clearances and Restrained Isolation Systems

Restrained isolation systems require adequate clearance for expected movement while maintaining the necessary restraint function. Incorrect clearance can cause premature contact and transmit impact forces into the structure.

Installation drawings should clearly communicate mounting orientation, support locations, restraint geometry, and movement requirements so field crews can reproduce the intended configuration.

Codes, Standards, and Engineering Requirements

Vibration isolation is primarily a dynamic engineering discipline, but equipment-support systems often intersect with structural and seismic requirements. Codes should therefore be incorporated according to the specific project scope rather than presented as a blanket certification for every isolator.

ASCE 7 and Seismic Requirements

ASCE 7 establishes structural loading provisions used in U.S. building design, including seismic provisions relevant to equipment and nonstructural components. Where mechanical equipment is subject to seismic design requirements, the restraint and anchorage strategy should be evaluated according to the applicable project criteria.

IBC and CBC

The International Building Code and California Building Code establish requirements that can affect building systems, structural supports, anchorage, and nonstructural components.

For California construction, coordination with the CBC and applicable project-specific requirements is particularly important when vibration isolation is combined with seismic restraint.

OSHPD/HCAI Projects

Healthcare projects under HCAI jurisdiction may involve detailed requirements for equipment supports, seismic restraint, documentation, and review. The terminology OSHPD remains common in industry discussions, while HCAI is the current agency name.

The presence of an isolation system does not eliminate the need to evaluate applicable seismic requirements. Engineering calculations and project documentation should reflect the actual equipment, support configuration, loads, and governing criteria.

Engineering and Custom Fabrication for Vibration Isolation Systems

Complex vibration-control projects often require more than an off-the-shelf mounting component. Equipment geometry, structural conditions, loading, seismic requirements, and MEP coordination can create situations where engineered support assemblies or custom hardware are appropriate.

Seismic Calculations and Structural Engineering

Engineering analysis can evaluate equipment loads, support conditions, seismic forces, anchorage, and restraint configurations. This is particularly valuable when isolated equipment must remain compatible with seismic design requirements.

BIM and 3D CAD modeling can help coordinate equipment, isolation components, support frames, structural interfaces, piping, ductwork, and surrounding MEP systems before fabrication or installation.

For complex mechanical rooms and industrial environments, three-dimensional coordination can identify clearance conflicts and connection problems that may not be obvious in two-dimensional drawings.

Custom Metal Fabrication

Custom fabrication can support applications requiring equipment frames, brackets, mounting plates, structural supports, custom strut channels, or other hardware.

Fabrication capabilities involving carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum, structural steel, sheet metal, welding, forming, machining, galvanizing, and powder coating can be incorporated according to project requirements.

The value of this approach is not simply producing a custom component. It is connecting engineering requirements, geometry, material selection, fabrication, and field installation into a coherent support solution.

Conclusion: Designing Vibration Isolation as an Integrated Engineering System

Effective vibration isolation begins with understanding the source of vibration, the transmission path, and the dynamic relationship between the equipment and its supporting structure. A successful system is not defined simply by choosing a spring, rubber mount, wire rope isolator, or acoustic hanger with an adequate load rating. The isolator must be compatible with equipment weight, load distribution, operating frequency, natural frequency, static deflection, damping, environmental conditions, movement requirements, and mounting geometry.

For HVAC and MEP systems, this means considering fans, pumps, chillers, compressors, air handling units, piping, ductwork, and connected services as an integrated installation. For industrial machinery, the analysis may need to account for rotating or reciprocating forces, machine foundations, operating cycles, environmental exposure, and process-specific vibration criteria. In healthcare, laboratory, data center, aerospace, and other sensitive facilities, the required performance may demand even closer coordination between equipment, structure, and building systems.

Seismic requirements add another layer. Vibration isolation and seismic restraint should not be treated as interchangeable functions. Isolators address dynamic vibration transmission, while seismic restraints and anchorage address earthquake-induced movement and forces. When both are required, the systems must be engineered together so seismic restraints do not unnecessarily compromise the intended isolation behavior.

For projects involving IBC, CBC, ASCE 7, or HCAI-related requirements, the appropriate design criteria should be established from the specific equipment and project conditions.

Ultimately, the strongest vibration isolation strategy is one that begins with measurable engineering requirements and carries those requirements through selection, detailing, fabrication, installation, and verification. That process provides a more reliable foundation for controlling transmitted vibration while maintaining the structural, mechanical, and seismic performance expected in demanding U.S. construction and industrial environments.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vibration Isolation

What is vibration isolation?

Vibration isolation is an engineering method for reducing the transmission of dynamic forces and vibration between equipment and its supporting structure. An isolation system introduces a controlled mechanical interface, such as a spring, elastomeric mount, wire rope isolator, or acoustic hanger, between the vibration source and the structure.

The effectiveness of the system depends on the relationship between the equipment's excitation frequency and the isolation system's dynamic characteristics. Equipment weight, stiffness, damping, static deflection, and mounting geometry all influence the resulting response.

How do vibration isolation systems work?

A vibration isolation system changes the mechanical path between equipment and its support. Instead of transferring equipment forces directly through a rigid connection, the isolator provides controlled compliance.

When properly selected, the isolator can reduce transmitted dynamic forces. Its natural frequency should be considered relative to the equipment's operating frequency because isolation behavior changes significantly around resonance.

The system also includes everything connected to the equipment. Rigid piping, ductwork, conduit, or structural connections can create bypass paths that reduce the effectiveness of an otherwise appropriate isolator.

What are the main types of vibration isolators?

Common technologies include spring vibration isolators, wire rope isolators, rubber and elastomeric mounts, rubber/metal isolators, acoustic hangers, floor-mounted isolation systems, and captive isolators.

Springs are frequently considered where higher static deflection and lower natural frequency are required. Elastomeric mounts can provide compact solutions for equipment with moderate isolation requirements. Wire rope systems can be useful in applications involving shock, vibration, and demanding environmental conditions.

There is no universally superior isolator type. Selection depends on equipment characteristics, operating frequency, loading, environmental exposure, movement requirements, and installation conditions.

How do I choose the right vibration isolation mount?

Start with the equipment rather than the mount. Important inputs include operating weight, center of gravity, individual support-point reactions, RPM, excitation frequencies, required static deflection, environmental conditions, available space, and the desired vibration-control performance.

Variable-speed equipment deserves particular attention because its excitation frequency can change during operation. The isolation system should also be checked for compatibility with the expected equipment movement and connected services.

For projects with seismic requirements, seismic restraint and anchorage should be evaluated at the same time rather than added after the isolation system has already been finalized.

Are spring isolators better than rubber vibration isolators?

Neither is inherently better. Spring and rubber isolation technologies have different stiffness, damping, deflection, load-capacity, frequency-response, and environmental characteristics.

Spring isolators may be advantageous for applications requiring relatively high static deflection and low natural frequency. Elastomeric mounts can be useful where compact geometry, damping, and moderate isolation requirements are important.

The correct choice depends on the application. A technically sound specification should identify the required performance and operating conditions instead of prescribing an isolator type without considering the equipment.

What is static deflection in vibration isolation?

Static deflection is the amount an isolator compresses or deflects under the supported load. It is particularly important for spring systems because static deflection is related to isolator stiffness and natural frequency.

If the equipment load is distributed unevenly, individual isolators can experience different deflections. This is why equipment weight should not automatically be divided equally among all mounting points.

Static deflection should be considered alongside operating frequency, allowable movement, equipment stability, and installation clearance.

What is the difference between vibration isolation and seismic restraint?

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint have different purposes. Vibration isolation is intended to reduce dynamic force transmission and vibration during equipment operation. Seismic restraint is intended to resist equipment movement and forces associated with earthquake loading.

An HVAC unit, pump, or other mechanical system may require both. In such cases, the restraint system must be coordinated with the isolation system so it provides the required seismic function without unnecessarily creating a rigid vibration-transmission path.

Applicable project requirements, including those associated with ASCE 7, IBC, CBC, and HCAI projects, should be evaluated by the design team.

Does vibration isolation apply to HVAC equipment?

Yes. HVAC equipment is a major application for vibration isolation. Fans, air handling units, pumps, chillers, compressors, cooling equipment, and other mechanical systems can generate dynamic forces that travel into building structures.

The isolation approach depends on equipment size, operating speed, support configuration, static loading, structural conditions, and required performance.

Flexible connections may also be required where piping and ductwork would otherwise bypass the isolation interface. Electrical and other service connections should similarly be coordinated with the equipment's expected movement.

Do vibration isolators need seismic restraints?

They may. Whether isolated equipment requires seismic restraint depends on the project, equipment, location, governing design criteria, and applicable code requirements.

A vibration isolator should not automatically be considered a seismic restraint. Conversely, adding rigid seismic restraints without considering isolation movement can compromise the intended vibration-control system.

Where both functions are required, the isolation and seismic restraint should be designed as an integrated equipment-support system.

Can vibration isolation be used for industrial machinery?

Yes. Industrial applications include motors, pumps, compressors, rotating machinery, manufacturing equipment, machine tools, and other equipment that produces dynamic excitation.

Industrial isolation often requires closer attention to operating speed, dynamic forces, process conditions, machine foundations, environmental exposure, and equipment alignment.

Isolation should also not be used as a substitute for correcting mechanical problems such as imbalance, misalignment, or deteriorated bearings. Source correction and isolation can be complementary parts of an effective vibration-control strategy.

When is custom vibration isolation fabrication necessary?

Custom fabrication can be useful when standard isolation components cannot adequately accommodate equipment geometry, support-point locations, structural interfaces, load requirements, clearance constraints, or project-specific connection details.

Custom equipment frames, mounting plates, brackets, structural supports, and strut-channel assemblies can be fabricated to integrate isolation components with the surrounding structure.

For complex projects, engineering, BIM/CAD coordination, and fabrication should be considered together. This approach helps ensure that the fabricated component reflects the actual loading and installation conditions rather than functioning as an isolated hardware selection.

Can vibration isolation systems be used in healthcare facilities?

Yes. Healthcare facilities commonly contain mechanical equipment and sensitive spaces where vibration transmission requires careful consideration. Hospitals and other healthcare environments can also involve additional structural, seismic, documentation, and review requirements.

Projects under HCAI jurisdiction should account for applicable project criteria and equipment-support requirements. The design should consider mechanical equipment, structural supports, seismic restraint, connected services, and installation conditions as a coordinated system.

For healthcare, laboratory, and other sensitive environments, vibration criteria should be established from the actual application rather than assuming that a particular isolator type provides a universal performance level.
